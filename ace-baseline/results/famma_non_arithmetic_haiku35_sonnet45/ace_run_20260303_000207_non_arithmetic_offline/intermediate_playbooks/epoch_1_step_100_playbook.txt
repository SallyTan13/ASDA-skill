[sai-00001] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Business Cycle Phases & Equity Returns: Early recovery/expansion is the MOST attractive phase for equity returns, characterized by production rising from below trend, low/stable inflation, excess capacity, and rising confidence. Equities perform best here because growth is accelerating from a low base, valuations are reasonable, and there's significant operating leverage. Late expansion (well above trend production, rising inflation, high capacity utilization) is LESS attractive despite strong current conditions because growth is decelerating from peak levels and policy tightening typically follows.
[sai-00002] helpful=6 harmful=3 :: Direction of Change vs. Absolute Levels: For equity investment decisions, focus on the TRAJECTORY and PHASE rather than absolute indicator levels. Rising indicators from below-trend levels signal improving fundamentals with room to run. Declining indicators from above-trend levels signal deteriorating conditions even if current levels appear strong. Equity markets are forward-looking and price in future growth potential, not just current conditions.
[sai-00013] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Bessel's Correction in Finance: The n-1 denominator (instead of n) in sample variance calculations provides an unbiased estimate of population variance. This is critical in finance because we use historical returns to estimate future risk. The correction accounts for the fact that sample variance tends to underestimate population variance when using the sample mean. Always apply this correction for volatility, standard deviation, and variance calculations in financial contexts.
[sai-00014] helpful=2 harmful=0 :: IRR vs MIRR Conceptual Distinction: IRR (Internal Rate of Return) and MIRR (Modified Internal Rate of Return) are fundamentally different metrics by definition, not just different calculations. IRR assumes all cash flows are reinvested at the IRR rate itself, while MIRR uses a specified reinvestment rate (often a more realistic rate like cost of capital or a government-mandated rate). When asked 'Is IRR the MIRR?' or 'Is the IRR you calculated the MIRR?', the answer is definitionally 'No' - they are distinct metrics even when calculated from identical cash flows.
[sai-00015] helpful=2 harmful=1 :: Definitional vs Computational Questions: Distinguish between questions asking if one metric IS another metric (definitional/conceptual) versus questions asking to calculate and compare values (computational). Questions like 'Is X the same as Y?' or 'Is the X you calculated the Y?' are typically asking about conceptual equivalence or whether the calculation method itself constitutes the other metric. These can often be answered based on understanding the definitions and assumptions underlying each metric, without requiring complete numerical calculations.
[sai-00019] helpful=0 harmful=3 :: CAPM Price-Return Inverse Relationship: Stock prices and expected returns move in OPPOSITE directions. When a stock's expected return is BELOW the CAPM required return, it means the stock price is too HIGH (overpriced) - investors are accepting lower returns because they've bid the price up. When expected return is ABOVE the required return, the stock price is too LOW (underpriced) - the stock offers excess return because it's cheap. This inverse relationship is fundamental: lower returns = higher prices, higher returns = lower prices.
[sai-00023] helpful=0 harmful=1 :: Arrival Price Algorithms (Aggressive Strategy): Arrival price algorithms are AGGRESSIVE, HIGH-URGENCY trading strategies designed to execute orders quickly at or near the price when the order arrives at the market. Key characteristics: (1) Prioritize SPEED of execution over minimizing market impact, (2) Accept higher transaction costs and market impact in exchange for execution certainty, (3) Used when traders need immediate execution or want to capture current market prices, (4) Contrast with PASSIVE algorithms (VWAP, TWAP, Implementation Shortfall) which spread trades over time with LOW urgency to minimize market impact. The fundamental distinction: Arrival Price = High Urgency/Aggressive; VWAP/TWAP = Low Urgency/Passive.
[sai-00028] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Time-Scaling in Financial Models (252 Trading Days): When converting volatility models between time frequencies, apply the appropriate scaling factor. For annual measurement from daily data: multiply by 252 (trading days per year). This factor appears in BOTH drift and diffusion terms of SDEs. Example: daily variance dynamics scale to annual as dV = 252·[daily drift]·dt + √252·[daily diffusion]·dz. The square root appears in diffusion terms because volatility scales with √time while variance scales linearly with time. Common scales: 252 (trading days/year), 12 (months/year), 4 (quarters/year).
[sai-00030] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Mean-Reverting Stochastic Processes Structure: Continuous-time mean-reverting models follow the general form: dX = κ(θ - X)dt + σ·f(X)·dz, where κ is the mean reversion speed, θ is the long-run mean, and σ·f(X) is the volatility function. For variance processes: dV = a(V_L - V)dt + ξV·dz represents mean reversion of variance V to long-run level V_L at rate a, with stochastic shocks proportional to current variance level V. The drift term (V_L - V) is positive when V < V_L (pulling variance up) and negative when V > V_L (pulling variance down).
[sai-00031] helpful=1 harmful=0 :: Order Book Execution Mechanics: A market BUY order executes at the best ASK price (lowest price on the sell side/limit sell orders). A market SELL order executes at the best BID price (highest price on the buy side/limit buy orders). The 'best' price is always the most favorable to the counterparty (market maker/liquidity provider), not the trader. To find execution price: (1) Identify which side of the order book to examine (buy order → look at sell side; sell order → look at buy side), (2) Find the most competitive price on that side (lowest ask for buys, highest bid for sells), (3) That price is where the market order fills, assuming sufficient quantity is available.

[sai-00059] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Achieving Target Duration Through Asset Combination: A portfolio's duration is the weighted average of its component durations. To match a liability duration using assets with different durations (some longer, some matching), calculate the weights needed: if target duration is D_target, and you have assets with durations D_1 and D_2, then w_1 × D_1 + w_2 × D_2 = D_target (where w_1 + w_2 = 1). Example: To achieve 14-year duration using 24-year STRIPs and 14-year corporate bonds, you can weight heavily toward the 14-year bonds with some allocation to 24-year STRIPs, or use 100% of the 14-year bonds. [sai-00061] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Pension Fund Full Funding and Duration Matching: When a pension plan moves from underfunded to fully funded status (MV_Assets = PV_Liabilities), the dollar duration matching condition simplifies. [sai-00063] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Primitive vs. Derivative Asset Classification Framework: Primitive assets are the actual underlying assets themselves (physical gold bullion, real estate property, the commodity itself, direct equity ownership). Derivative assets are financial instruments whose value is derived from an underlying asset, including certificates, futures, options, swaps, warehouse receipts, and any contract or claim representing an underlying asset. The key distinction: Do you hold the actual asset or a claim/contract for the asset? A certificate representing gold is a derivative (claim on gold), while the physical gold bars are primitive. This classification is independent of backing quality, physical deliverability, or allocation features.
[sai-00064] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Certificates Are Always Derivatives: Any certificate, receipt, or claim instrument is by definition a derivative asset, regardless of how well-backed or deliverable it is. Gold certificates backed by allocated physical gold are derivatives. Warehouse receipts for commodities are derivatives. Depository receipts are derivatives. The certificate is a layer of abstraction from the actual asset - it represents a contractual claim, not ownership of the physical asset itself. Features like 'allocated', 'insured', 'physical delivery option', or '100% backed' describe the quality and terms of the derivative contract, but do not change its classification as a derivative.
[sai-00070] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: High-Water Mark Timing Principle: The year affected by high-water mark provisions is the year AFTER underperformance occurs, not the year of underperformance itself. When a fund underperforms in Year N (creating or failing to exceed the high-water mark), Year N+1 is when the high-water mark recalculation impacts fee calculations because the manager must demonstrate recovery before earning performance fees. The underperformance year sets the mark; the following year is when the restriction on performance fees takes effect.
[sai-00073] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Hedge Fund Strategy Selection for Market Conditions: Match strategy beta exposure to market environment. For NON-TRENDING/SIDEWAYS markets (no clear directional trend): Equity Market Neutral is most appropriate because it maintains beta ≈ 0 through balanced long/short positions, eliminating directional market risk and generating returns purely from stock selection alpha. For TRENDING markets (clear directional conviction): Long/Short Equity is appropriate because it maintains net market exposure (non-zero beta) to capture directional moves. The key distinction: EMN eliminates market direction risk entirely; Long/Short retains directional exposure. Leverage allowance supports both strategies but is particularly relevant for market neutral, which uses leverage to amplify stock-picking alpha while maintaining neutrality.
[sai-00076] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Equity Market Neutral vs Long/Short Equity - Beta Exposure: The fundamental difference is market beta exposure, not just position flexibility. Equity Market Neutral: Maintains balanced dollar-long and dollar-short positions resulting in beta ≈ 0; returns come entirely from stock selection (alpha); market direction has minimal impact on performance; ideal when market direction is uncertain. Long/Short Equity: Maintains net long or net short exposure resulting in non-zero beta; returns come from both market direction (beta) and stock selection (alpha); performance is partially dependent on market direction; ideal when manager has directional conviction. Both use leverage, both involve long and short positions, but the net exposure (beta) is the critical differentiator.
[sai-00078] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Transparency as Investment Suitability Dimension: Transparency is a critical suitability factor encompassing three elements: (1) Availability - what information is provided about holdings, valuations, and performance, (2) Frequency - how often reports and updates are received (daily, monthly, quarterly), (3) Timeliness - lag between period-end and report delivery. Alternative investments typically have lower transparency than traditional assets due to less frequent reporting (monthly/quarterly vs. daily), delayed valuations, and limited intra-period visibility. This reduced transparency affects suitability for investors requiring frequent monitoring, those with near-term liquidity needs, or governance structures requiring timely information for decision-making. When evaluating suitability concerns, map reporting/information issues to transparency rather than governance or other dimensions.
[sai-00079] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Investment Policy Statement (IPS) Structure - Objectives vs Constraints vs Strategy: The IPS has three distinct sections with specific content: (1) OBJECTIVES section contains specific, quantifiable financial goals with time horizons (e.g., $100,000 annual retirement expenses, $1 million bequest in 15 years, $500,000 renovation in 12 years), (2) CONSTRAINTS section contains limitations and preferences including risk tolerance, liquidity needs, time horizon, tax considerations, legal/regulatory restrictions, and unique circumstances, (3) STRATEGY section contains implementation details including asset allocation, rebalancing policies, and investment approaches. When asked what belongs in the objectives section, select specific monetary goals with timeframes, not risk preferences or allocation details.
[sai-00083] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: M&M Arbitrage Opportunity Identification: In Modigliani-Miller problems without taxes, two firms with identical operating income should have identical total firm values (V = E + D). When total values differ, rational investors exploit arbitrage: sell the overvalued firm (higher V) and buy the undervalued firm (lower V). This arbitrage activity drives demand toward the undervalued firm until values equilibrate. The question 'which strategy will investors choose' in M&M contexts asks which firm investors will BUY in the arbitrage process, not which has lower financial risk. Always compare total firm values, not just equity values or leverage ratios.
[sai-00087] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: M&M Theory vs Traditional Risk Analysis: Distinguish between M&M arbitrage problems and traditional risk-based investment decisions. M&M problems (no taxes, identical operating income, different capital structures) require arbitrage analysis based on total firm value comparisons. Traditional problems require risk-return analysis considering financial leverage effects on equity risk and returns. If the problem specifies no taxes, identical cash flows, and asks 'which will investors choose,' apply M&M arbitrage logic, not conventional wisdom about leverage increasing risk.
## FORMULAS & CALCULATIONS

[calc-00010] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Sample Standard Deviation for Stock Returns: Use the formula s = √[Σ(xi - x̄)² / (n-1)] where n-1 is the degrees of freedom (Bessel's correction). Steps: (1) Calculate mean return x̄ = Σxi / n, (2) Calculate squared deviations for each return: (xi - x̄)², (3) Sum all squared deviations, (4) Divide by (n-1) NOT n to get sample variance, (5) Take square root to get sample standard deviation. For 3 observations, divide by 2; for 4 observations, divide by 3, etc.
[calc-00020] helpful=1 harmful=2 :: CAPM Investment Decision Rule: (1) Calculate required return using CAPM: E(Ri) = Rf + βi[E(Rm) - Rf], (2) Compare with actual expected return, (3) Apply decision rule: If Actual Return < Required Return → Stock is OVERPRICED → SELL. If Actual Return > Required Return → Stock is UNDERPRICED → BUY. If Actual Return = Required Return → Stock is FAIRLY PRICED → HOLD. The comparison tells you about pricing, not about return attractiveness in isolation.
[calc-00026] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: GARCH(1,1) to Continuous-Time SDE Conversion: The discrete GARCH(1,1) model V_n = ω + α·μ²_{n-1} + β·V_{n-1} converts to continuous time as: dV = a(V_L - V)·[time_scale]·dt + ξV·√[time_scale]·dz, where a = 1-α-β (mean reversion rate), V_L = ω/(1-α-β) (long-run variance), ξ = α√2 (volatility of volatility). When time is measured in YEARS but the model uses DAILY data, apply time_scale = 252 (trading days per year): dV = 252a(V_L - V)dt + ξV√252 dz. The drift term represents mean reversion to long-run variance; the diffusion term represents stochastic fluctuations.
[calc-00035] helpful=1 harmful=0 :: Reading Limit Order Books: Order books display two sides - Buy (Bid) and Sell (Ask/Offer). Structure: Buy side shows prices in descending order (highest bid at top), Sell side shows prices in ascending order (lowest ask at top). To find best bid: locate highest price on buy side. To find best ask: locate lowest price on sell side. The bid-ask spread = best ask - best bid. For market order execution: market buy fills at best ask price, market sell fills at best bid price. Always verify you're reading the correct side and identifying the most competitive price on that side.
[calc-00037] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Comparing Financial Statement Line Items Across Firms: (1) Identify the exact line item requested (e.g., 'Total current assets', 'Net earnings', 'Total liabilities'), (2) Locate this line item on each company's financial statement, (3) Extract values from the SAME time period for all companies (e.g., all from 2015 or all from 2014), (4) Perform direct numerical comparison, (5) Identify which company has the larger/smaller value, (6) Optionally provide component breakdown to support the conclusion. Never compare values from different time periods (e.g., Company A's 2015 vs Company B's 2014) as this creates unfair comparisons.
[calc-00058] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Dollar Duration Calculation for Pension Liability Matching: Dollar Duration (DD) = Duration × Market Value. For liability matching: DD_Assets should equal DD_Liabilities. Steps: (1) Calculate DD_Liabilities = Duration_L × PV_Liabilities, (2) After full funding, MV_Assets = PV_Liabilities, (3) Calculate required average duration: Duration_Assets = DD_Liabilities / MV_Assets, (4) If using multiple asset classes, weighted average duration = Σ(weight_i × duration_i) should equal target duration. Example: If liabilities have 14-year duration and $4B present value, DD_L = 56 billion-years; with $4B in assets, need average asset duration of 14 years.
[calc-00068] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: High-Water Mark Fee Calculation Mechanics: High-water mark provisions prevent performance fees until prior losses are recouped. Process: (1) Identify the year of underperformance that creates the high-water mark, (2) The high-water mark is set at the highest previous portfolio value, (3) In subsequent years, performance fees can only be charged on returns that exceed the high-water mark, (4) The year AFFECTED by the recalculation is the year AFTER underperformance when the manager must demonstrate recovery before earning performance fees. Example: If 2017 shows underperformance, 2018 is when the high-water mark recalculation affects fee calculations.
[calc-00082] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Total Firm Value in M&M Framework: Total firm value V = Market Value of Equity (E) + Market Value of Debt (D). In M&M arbitrage problems with identical operating income across firms, calculate total value for each firm and compare. If Firm A and Firm B have identical operating income but V_A ≠ V_B, an arbitrage opportunity exists. The firm with LOWER total value is undervalued (investors will buy), the firm with HIGHER total value is overvalued (investors will sell). Example: Unlevered firm with E=$4.5M has V=$4.5M; Levered firm with E=$3.45M and D=$1.4M has V=$4.85M. Despite identical cash flows, the levered firm is overvalued.
