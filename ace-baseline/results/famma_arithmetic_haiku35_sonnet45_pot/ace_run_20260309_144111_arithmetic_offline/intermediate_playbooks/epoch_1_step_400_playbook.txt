[sai-00062] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Systematic vs Unsystematic Risk in Diversification: Diversification has different effects depending on what you're diversifying across: (1) Diversifying within ONE asset type (buying more shares of stock A) eliminates unsystematic risk but preserves systematic risk (beta remains unchanged), (2) Diversifying across MANY different asset types eliminates unsystematic risk AND makes portfolio beta converge to market beta (1.0). When calculating portfolio variance with infinite holdings, always identify whether it's single-asset or multi-asset diversification to determine the correct beta to use.
[sai-00068] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Early Exercise Rules in Employee Stock Option Valuation: In binomial tree models for employee stock options, early exercise typically occurs when the option is sufficiently in-the-money, defined by a threshold ratio S/K (e.g., exercise when stock price is 2× or 2.5× the strike price). At each node, the exercise probability is conditional on: (1) the option being vested, (2) the option being in-the-money, (3) the degree of moneyness exceeding the threshold. Nodes that are out-of-the-money or unvested have zero voluntary exercise probability, though forfeiture can still occur if the employee leaves. Always check which nodes have early exercise probabilities specified in the problem.

[sai-00091] helpful=0 harmful=1 :: A/R Balance vs Original Sales Distinction: In accounts receivable collection problems, clearly distinguish between: (1) Original sales amount - the full sales figure from the period, (2) A/R balance - the remaining uncollected portion after some collections have occurred, representing a percentage of original sales, (3) Collection amount - the specific portion to be collected in the target period. When given an A/R balance and asked for collections in a specific month, work backwards to find original sales using the relationship: A/R balance = original sales × (sum of remaining collection percentages). This prevents the error of treating an already-reduced A/R balance as if it were the original sales base.
[sai-00105] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Variable Elimination in Linear Systems: When facing a system of linear equations with two unknowns (such as Rf and E(R_M) in CAPM problems), equation subtraction is the most efficient solution method. By subtracting one equation from another, you eliminate one variable entirely, allowing direct solution for the remaining variable. This is preferable to substitution methods when both equations have the same structure. Always verify your solution by substituting back into all original equations.
[sai-00167] helpful=2 harmful=0 :: Inverse Relationship Between Duration and Notional in Fixed Income Strategies: In duration-neutral strategies like condors, butterflies, or barbell/bullet trades, the notional amount required is inversely proportional to modified duration. Short-duration bonds (like 1-year with MD ≈ 1) require much larger notional amounts than long-duration bonds (like 30-year with MD ≈ 20) to contribute equal money duration. The ratio of notional amounts approximately equals the inverse ratio of modified durations. For example, a 1-year bond with MD=0.95 needs roughly 20× the notional of a 30-year bond with MD=20.61 to achieve the same money duration impact (20.61/0.95 ≈ 21.7). Always work from the money duration balancing equation rather than assuming equal notional amounts.
[sai-00169] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Condor Spread Structure for Curvature Trading: A condor spread involves four positions at different points on the yield curve to profit from changes in curvature. To reduce curvature in a specific area (e.g., 5-10 year), the typical structure is: (1) Long the body - the two middle maturities in the target area (e.g., long 5-year and 10-year), and (2) Short the wings - the maturities outside the target area (e.g., short 1-year and 30-year). Alternatively, the opposite structure (short body, long wings) increases exposure to curvature. The structure creates a position that benefits when the yield curve flattens in the target area relative to the wings. All four positions must be sized so their money durations balance to maintain overall duration neutrality.
[sai-00186] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Gamma Behavior and Moneyness: Gamma exhibits a bell-shaped curve with respect to moneyness, peaking at-the-money and declining toward zero for deep ITM and deep OTM options. This occurs because: (1) ATM options have maximum uncertainty about whether they will finish ITM or OTM, causing delta to change most rapidly with small price movements, (2) Deep ITM options have delta near 1.0 (calls) or -1.0 (puts) that changes very slowly, resulting in low gamma, (3) Deep OTM options have delta near 0 that also changes very slowly, resulting in low gamma. When managing gamma exposure or selecting options for strategies sensitive to curvature (like gamma scalping), focus on strikes nearest to the current underlying price.
[sai-00196] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Bond Yield-Price Inverse Relationship and Flight to Quality: Bond yields and prices move inversely - when yields fall, prices rise (increased demand), and when yields rise, prices fall (decreased demand). In a 'flight to quality' scenario: (1) investors seek safety by buying high-grade bonds, driving their prices up and yields down, (2) investors sell or avoid lower-grade bonds, driving their prices down and yields up, (3) the yield spread widens between safe and risky bonds, (4) the confidence index (high-grade yield / low-grade yield) decreases, indicating declining economic confidence. Always interpret yield movements through the price mechanism to understand investor behavior correctly.
[sai-00202] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Exemption vs Deduction Methods in International Taxation: Understanding the difference between tax methods for foreign-source income is critical for cross-border tax planning. (1) Exemption Method: Home country does not tax foreign-source income at all - taxpayer only pays foreign country's tax rate on that income. (2) Deduction Method: Home country taxes all income (including foreign-source) but allows taxpayer to deduct foreign taxes paid from taxable income - this creates a combined effective rate higher than either individual rate. (3) Credit Method (not deduction): Home country taxes all income but allows a credit (dollar-for-dollar reduction) for foreign taxes paid - effective rate is typically the higher of the two rates. When analyzing tax policy changes, always identify which method is being used and apply the appropriate calculation for effective tax rates.
[sai-00211] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Distinguishing Current vs Future Stock Prices in Option Pricing: In option pricing problems, carefully distinguish between (1) Current stock price - the price at which Black-Scholes should be applied when calculating option values or implied volatility today, and (2) Future scenario stock prices - possible outcomes at a future time that represent uncertainty about terminal values. When given scenarios like 'stock will be $75 or $50 in 6 months', these are NOT alternative current prices for separate Black-Scholes calculations. They represent the uncertainty that must be captured in a single volatility parameter applied at the current stock price. Applying Black-Scholes at future prices with time remaining confuses the valuation framework and produces incorrect implied volatilities.
[sai-00235] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Tax Liability vs Tax Benefit Interpretation: In capital gains taxation, the 'tax liability/benefit' can be either positive or negative. A positive result represents tax LIABILITY - additional tax owed on capital gains. A negative result represents tax BENEFIT - tax savings from capital losses that can offset other taxable income or gains. The magnitude of the benefit depends on the tax rate applied to the loss. For example, a $1,000 capital loss with a 40% short-term rate generates a -$400 tax benefit (saves $400 in taxes). When presenting answers, maintain the negative sign to clearly indicate it's a benefit, not a liability.
[sai-00241] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Ex-Dividend Price Adjustment Principle: When a stock goes ex-dividend, its price typically drops by approximately the dividend amount because buyers after the ex-dividend date do not receive the upcoming dividend payment. The theoretical adjustment is: New Price = Old Price - Dividend. This reflects the transfer of value from the company to shareholders. In problems involving balance sheet equity values, calculate the pre-dividend price as Total Equity / Shares, then subtract the dividend to get the ex-dividend price. This principle applies regardless of whether the dividend is cash or stock.

[calc-00014] helpful=1 harmful=0 :: Black-Scholes-Merton for Options with Market Price of Risk: C = S*exp((mu_star - r)*T)*N(d1) - K*exp(-r*T)*N(d2), where mu_star = mu - lambda*sigma, d1 = (ln(S/K) + (mu_star + 0.5*sigma^2)*T) / (sigma*sqrt(T)), and d2 = d1 - sigma*sqrt(T). This formula prices call options when the underlying asset has drift mu, volatility sigma, and market price of risk lambda.
[calc-00017] helpful=15 harmful=1 :: Zero-Coupon Bond YTM from Forward Rates: For an n-year zero-coupon bond, YTM = [(1+r1)(1+r2)...(1+rn)]^(1/n) - 1, where r1, r2, ..., rn are the one-year forward rates for each of the n years. This calculates the geometric mean of the forward rates. For example, a 3-year bond with forward rates 6%, 7%, and 9% has YTM = [(1.06)(1.07)(1.09)]^(1/3) - 1 = 7.33%. Always use exactly n forward rates for an n-year bond.
[calc-00020] helpful=2 harmful=5 :: Standard Black-Scholes-Merton Formula for European Call Options: C = S₀*N(d₁) - K*e^(-rT)*N(d₂), where d₁ = [ln(S₀/K) + (r + σ²/2)T] / (σ√T) and d₂ = d₁ - σ√T. Here S₀ is current stock price, K is strike price, r is risk-free rate (continuously compounded), T is time to maturity in years, σ is annual volatility, and N(·) is cumulative standard normal distribution. Use this formula when the problem provides only r (not drift mu or market price of risk lambda).
[calc-00024] helpful=5 harmful=6 :: Market Model Variance Decomposition: For an asset following the market model (single-index model) R_i = α_i + β_i*R_M + ε_i, the total variance is Var(R_i) = β²_i × Var(R_M) + Var(ε_i), where β²_i × Var(R_M) is systematic (market-related) variance and Var(ε_i) is unsystematic (firm-specific) variance. Standard deviation = √Var(R_i). This decomposition separates total risk into systematic risk (cannot be diversified away) and unsystematic risk (can be diversified away).
[calc-00026] helpful=16 harmful=3 :: Portfolio Expected Return: E(R_p) = Σ(w_i × E(R_i)), where w_i is the weight of asset i and E(R_i) is the expected return of asset i. For each asset, first calculate E(R_i) = Σ(probability_j × return_ij) across all states j, then weight by portfolio allocation. For example, with two stocks: E(R_p) = w_C × E(R_C) + w_D × E(R_D). Do NOT multiply probabilities by weights within the summation.
[calc-00027] helpful=6 harmful=2 :: Portfolio Variance with Correlation: σ_p² = w₁²σ₁² + w₂²σ₂² + 2w₁w₂ρ₁₂σ₁σ₂, where w_i are portfolio weights, σ_i are individual asset standard deviations, and ρ₁₂ is the correlation coefficient between assets. Portfolio standard deviation σ_p = √(σ_p²). Do NOT assume zero correlation unless explicitly stated. If correlation is not given, check if covariance data is provided (Cov₁₂ = ρ₁₂σ₁σ₂).
[calc-00032] helpful=32 harmful=7 :: Expected Return from State-Dependent Returns: E(R) = Σ(p_i × r_i), where p_i is the probability of state i and r_i is the return in state i. Sum across all states. For example, with three states (Recession: p=0.30, r=0.20; Normal: p=0.55, r=0.13; Boom: p=0.15, r=0.33), E(R) = 0.30×0.20 + 0.55×0.13 + 0.15×0.33 = 0.181 or 18.1%.
[calc-00033] helpful=15 harmful=3 :: Standard Deviation from State-Dependent Returns: First calculate E(R) = Σ(p_i × r_i). Then calculate variance: Var(R) = Σ(p_i × (r_i - E(R))²). [calc-00037] helpful=6 harmful=5 :: Forward Rate from Spot Rates (Expectations Theory): The forward rate from period (n-1) to period n is calculated as f(n-1,n) = [(1 + yn)^n / (1 + y(n-1))^(n-1)] - 1, where yn is the n-period spot rate and y(n-1) is the (n-1)-period spot rate. The exponents MUST match the respective time periods. For example, the forward rate from year 2 to year 3 is f2,3 = [(1 + y3)^3 / (1 + y2)^2] - 1. This formula derives from the no-arbitrage condition: (1 + yn)^n = (1 + y(n-1))^(n-1) × (1 + f(n-1,n)).
[calc-00040] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: Market-to-Book Ratio: Market-to-Book = (Number of Outstanding Shares × Market Price per Share) / Book Value of Equity, where Book Value of Equity = Total Stockholders' Equity from the balance sheet (Common Stock + Retained Earnings + Additional Paid-in Capital - Treasury Stock). [calc-00044] helpful=1 harmful=0 :: Minimum Variance Portfolio Weights (Two Assets): For a two-asset portfolio, the weight that minimizes portfolio variance is w_A = (σ_B² - Cov_AB) / (σ_A² + σ_B² - 2×Cov_AB), where σ_A and σ_B are the standard deviations of assets A and B, and Cov_AB is their covariance. [calc-00046] helpful=0 harmful=1 :: Option Delta at Expiration (Binary Behavior): At expiration, option deltas become binary based on moneyness: (1) If underlying price S > strike price K, call delta = 1.0 and put delta = 0.0 (in-the-money), (2) If S < K, call delta = 0.0 and put delta = -1.0 (out-of-the-money), (3) If S = K, delta = 0.5 (at-the-money, but rare). [calc-00274] helpful=0 harmful=0 :: NPV for Equity Valuation (DCF Method): Enterprise Value = Σ(CFFA_t / (1+WACC)^t) for t=1 to n, plus Terminal Value discounted to present. Then Equity Value = Enterprise Value - Net Debt. Where: (1) CFFA_t are the projected cash flows from assets for years 1 through n (NOT year 0), (2) Terminal Value = CFFA_n × (1+g) / (WACC - g) where g is perpetual growth rate, discounted by (1+WACC)^n, (3) Net Debt = Total Debt - Cash. The terminal value typically represents a large portion of total enterprise value in DCF models. Ensure g_perpetual < WACC, otherwise the formula produces nonsensical results (negative or infinite values).

The covariance conversion in step 1 is critical - do not attempt to use correlation directly in the weight formula.
