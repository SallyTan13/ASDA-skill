# Skill Patterns for Economics Concept Confusion Errors

## Pattern: Exchange Rate Quotation Directionality

**Description:** Confusion about how currency depreciation/appreciation translates to exchange rate movements in different quotation conventions (direct vs. indirect). A DOM/FOR quotation means "units of domestic currency per unit of foreign currency," so domestic currency depreciation causes this rate to RISE, not fall.

**When to Use:** When analyzing exchange rate forecasts involving PPP, interest rate parity, or any currency valuation question. Trigger keywords: "DOM/FOR," "exchange rate," "depreciate," "appreciate," "currency weakening/strengthening."

**Procedure:**
1. Identify the exchange rate quotation convention: DOM/FOR means domestic currency units per one unit of foreign currency
2. Determine which currency is expected to depreciate/appreciate based on the economic theory (PPP, interest differentials, etc.)
3. Apply the directional rule: If domestic currency depreciates, DOM/FOR RISES (more domestic units needed per foreign unit); if domestic currency appreciates, DOM/FOR FALLS
4. Verify by checking the inverse: FOR/DOM would move in the opposite direction
5. Cross-check with inflation differentials: higher domestic inflation → domestic currency depreciates → DOM/FOR rises

**Example (sanitized):**
> **Scenario:** The EUR/USD rate is 1.20. European inflation is forecast at 4% while US inflation is 2%. What happens to EUR/USD under PPP?
> **Wrong approach:** "Higher European inflation means euro weakens, so EUR/USD falls."
> **Correct approach:** (1) EUR/USD means euros per dollar. (2) Higher European inflation causes euro to depreciate relative to dollar. (3) Euro depreciation means MORE euros needed per dollar. (4) Therefore EUR/USD RISES above 1.20. (5) Verify: USD/EUR would fall, confirming euro weakness.

## Pattern: Monetary Policy Trilemma (Impossible Trinity)

**Description:** Failure to apply the monetary policy trilemma, which states a country cannot simultaneously maintain: (1) fixed exchange rate, (2) free capital mobility, and (3) independent monetary policy. The combination of unrestricted capital flows AND fixed exchange rate creates the STRONGEST constraint on monetary independence.

**When to Use:** When evaluating monetary policy independence across countries with different exchange rate regimes and capital control policies. Trigger keywords: "monetary policy independence," "capital flows," "fixed/floating exchange rate," "capital controls," "restricted/unrestricted."

**Procedure:**
1. Identify each country's exchange rate regime (fixed, floating, or managed)
2. Identify each country's capital flow policy (restricted or unrestricted)
3. Apply trilemma logic: Countries with BOTH fixed exchange rates AND unrestricted capital flows have the LEAST monetary policy independence (must use interest rates to defend the peg)
4. Recognize that capital restrictions provide MORE policy flexibility by insulating from external flows
5. Rank independence: Floating + unrestricted (most independent) > Fixed + restricted > Fixed + unrestricted (least independent)
6. Verify: The country that must defend a fixed rate while capital can freely flow in/out faces maximum constraint

**Example (sanitized):**
> **Scenario:** Country A: floating rate, unrestricted capital. Country B: fixed rate, restricted capital. Country C: fixed rate, unrestricted capital. Which has least monetary independence?
> **Wrong approach:** "Country B has restricted capital, so it's least independent."
> **Correct approach:** (1) Country C has fixed rate AND unrestricted capital. (2) Under trilemma, this combination sacrifices monetary independence. (3) Country C must adjust interest rates to maintain the peg while capital flows freely respond to rate differentials. (4) Country B's capital restrictions provide insulation despite fixed rate. (5) Country A has full independence with floating rate. (6) Answer: Country C has least independence.

## Pattern: Currency Risk Premium and Bond Yield Direction

**Description:** Misunderstanding that when currency depreciation risk increases (e.g., peg at risk of breaking), bond yields must RISE to compensate investors for the additional risk, not fall. Bond prices and yields move inversely, and currency risk requires higher yields.

**When to Use:** When analyzing bond yields in the context of currency pegs, exchange rate risk, or sovereign risk changes. Trigger keywords: "bond yields," "currency peg," "at risk," "currency weakening," "depreciation risk."

**Procedure:**
1. Identify the currency risk scenario (peg breaking, expected depreciation, etc.)
2. Determine the direction of risk change: increased currency risk means investors face potential losses
3. Apply risk premium logic: Higher risk requires higher yields to attract/retain investors
4. Remember the inverse relationship: Higher yields mean lower bond prices
5. Verify: If a currency is expected to weaken, foreign investors demand higher yields to compensate for expected currency losses
6. Check for sovereign risk spillover: Currency stress often correlates with fiscal stress, further increasing required yields

**Example (sanitized):**
> **Scenario:** Country Z maintains a currency peg to the dollar. Market participants believe the peg may break with the currency depreciating 15%. What happens to Country Z's bond yields?
> **Wrong approach:** "Currency weakening means lower yields as the currency becomes less valuable."
> **Correct approach:** (1) Peg breaking risk increases uncertainty and potential losses for bondholders. (2) Investors face currency depreciation risk if they hold bonds denominated in Country Z's currency. (3) To compensate for this risk, investors demand higher yields. (4) Bond yields RISE as risk premium increases. (5) Verify: Higher yields reflect both currency risk and potential sovereign stress. (6) Bond prices fall as yields rise.

## Pattern: GDP Growth and Equity Returns Causality

**Description:** Confusing correlation with causation between GDP growth and equity market returns. While empirically correlated over long periods, the relationship is indirect (GDP → corporate earnings → equity returns) and not reliably causal due to factors like profit margin changes, valuation multiples, dividend policies, and differences between listed company composition and broader economy.

**When to Use:** When evaluating statements about the relationship between macroeconomic growth and asset class returns, particularly for equities. Trigger keywords: "GDP growth," "equity returns," "causally linked," "long-term relationship," "stock market performance."

**Procedure:**
1. Distinguish between correlation (observed co-movement) and causation (direct causal mechanism)
2. For GDP-equity claims, recognize the indirect pathway: GDP growth may influence corporate earnings, which may affect equity returns
3. Identify confounding factors: changes in profit margins, P/E multiples, payout ratios, composition of listed firms vs. economy, globalization of revenues
4. For bond yields-GDP claims, recognize the direct causal mechanism through real interest rates and monetary policy
5. Evaluate whether the statement claims "correlation" or "causation" and assess if the causal mechanism is theoretically sound
6. Accept only statements with robust theoretical causal support, not just empirical correlation

**Example (sanitized):**
> **Scenario:** Analyst makes two claims: (A) "Real bond yields are causally linked to trend GDP growth." (B) "Equity returns are causally linked to GDP growth." How many are accurate?
> **Wrong approach:** "Both show empirical relationships in data, so both are accurate."
> **Correct approach:** (1) Claim A: Real interest rates have theoretical causal link to economic growth through savings/investment and monetary policy channels—ACCURATE. (2) Claim B: While correlated, equity returns depend on earnings growth (not GDP), margin changes, multiple expansion/contraction, and global revenue exposure—relationship is correlation, not robust causation—INACCURATE. (3) Verify: Listed companies may grow earnings faster/slower than GDP due to sector composition, international operations, and financial engineering. (4) Answer: Only one statement (A) is accurate.
